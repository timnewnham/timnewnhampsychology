I — The Ordinary World

Growing up, I had no map.

I was raised in Australia, in a divorced household with no firm house to call home. The world around me not only expected boys to figure things out on their own, but it also vilified them for boisterous behaviour. Nobody talked about what it meant to become a man in positive form; there was no structure to follow or anyone to guide us into this role. You were just supposed to know.

I was missing that initiation, that trial to test my mettle. I worked as an electrician, and I remember gazing up at the moon one day and feeling overwhelmed with nerves and excitement; something was missing, a lot was missing. A sense of purpose, a challenge, an adventure, to prove my worth to myself and my community, and to figure out who I was as a man, and what I was capable of.

✦
II — The Call

That afternoon, as I gazed up at the moon, I applied to join the Australian Army.

I enlisted as an infantryman. What followed was years of physical and psychological pressure that helped me develop into who I am today. I underwent overseas deployment, leadership positions and reconnaissance unit postings. It was the first time in my life I face real adversity and encountered structure, a system that supported me and guided me towards embodying my masculine self.

It was here that I experienced brotherhood, the kind of connection that only forms by experiencing and overcoming shared hardship. I'd found my home, a place where I felt respected, and the world made sense to me again.

For the first time, I understood what it felt like to be respected as part of a broader team, to feel responsible and heard, and to represent something larger than myself.

But the military is not a complete initiation for young men. It asks you to complete the mission, but it doesn't care how you do it or how you end up afterwards. It connects you to your warrior archetype, but only through an immature pathway that hardens your shell, and leaves you dealing with built-up anger, and feeling emotionally and spiritually incomplete.

✦
III — The Road of Trials

Life afterwards had its own challenges to overcome

After returning from overseas deployment, it was time to hang up the boots and part ways. This transition period out of the military is disorienting as you navigate your way around finding your feet again. The structure disappears. The identity shifts. Your brotherhood, farewelled. The purpose, once clear, suddenly has no obvious replacement.

It was in this moment that I hit rock bottom. What would I do? Who would I become? What I really needed was a guide, someone to point the way, but I was then too proud to ask for help. After months of rumination, I bought a one-way ticket to South America and travelled northward for a year. For me it was a time of reflection and healing. A moment to re-connect with society outside of the military. I sat on many rocks in deep thought, read many books, and eventually was struck by a realisation. I had originally joined the Army to make the world a better place, but in reality it had only brought destructive outcomes, to others, and to myself. Now, it was time to actually help people, in a mature and regenerative way.

✦
IV — The Return

An integration of my experiences

Upon return to Australia, I decided I would begin my path towards helping make the world a better place by using the skills I already had. I started fitness and nutrition coaching, which brought me the satisfaction of watching other people grow. But something was missing, and I knew I was circling something deeper.

Looking around me, I saw that people weren't just struggling with their physical bodies, but were mentally and spiritually hollow. So I asked myself: what good am I doing guiding someone to exercise, if they lack meaning in their life and then in the evening binge eat to feel satisfied, or drink so much to disconnect from their reality?

All of that self-work and development I gained through my own struggles took me down a path of learning and self-discovery. Eventually I found myself living in the Netherlands, where I formally studied psychology at the University of Groningen, which eventually led to building the programme you're reading about now.

I am not a finished product. I am someone walking the same path, which is exactly what a guide is supposed to be.

If any of this resonates, I'd like to hear from you.
